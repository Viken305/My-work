# 72 Hours Without Sleep: narration and fact sheet

Voice: Kokoro `af_heart` (US female, 1.0×), the same narrator as the other channel Shorts. Narration lines live in
`script.py`; word timings come from the real audio (`timing.py`).

1. **Hook.** Stay awake for three days, and your brain will start showing you things that are not there.
   Here is what happens to your body, hour by hour.
2. **Hours 0–16.** Meet Sam. Sam woke up at seven this morning. And from that moment, a chemical called adenosine
   started building up in Sam's brain. Think of it as a jar of sleep pressure. Coffee can hide the feeling for a
   while. But the jar keeps filling. By four in the morning, Sam's body clock hits its lowest point. Body temperature
   drops, and the urge to sleep is at its strongest.
3. **Hour 24.** Twenty four hours awake. Sam's reaction time is now about as bad as someone with a blood alcohol level
   of zero point one percent. That is over the legal limit for driving.
   The brain's emotional alarm, the amygdala, becomes about sixty percent more reactive. Small things feel huge.
   Memory takes a hit too. The brain's memory center, the hippocampus, now takes in about forty percent less new
   information.
   And the hunger hormone rises, while the hormone that says you are full drops. Hello, junk food cravings.
4. **Hours 36–48.** Thirty six hours. Sam's body now treats this like an emergency. Stress hormones climb, and
   inflammation spreads. Even the brain's cleanup slows down. One study found a single sleepless night raised beta
   amyloid, a waste protein linked to Alzheimer's, by about five percent. Forty eight hours. Now the brain starts taking microsleeps. Tiny blackouts, a few seconds
   long. Sam's eyes can stay open. Sam won't even notice. At highway speed, a few seconds is the length of a
   football field.
5. **Hour 72 and after.** Seventy two hours. The walls seem to breathe. Shapes move at the edge of your vision. Many
   people start to hallucinate. The record? In 1964, a student named Randy Gardner stayed awake for eleven days.
   The good news? Sam doesn't have to pay back every lost hour. A night or two of deep sleep, and the jar drains.
   Most adults need seven to nine hours a night. So the next time you think about an all nighter, remember the jar.

## Fact sheet

| Claim | Check |
|---|---|
| Adenosine builds up while awake and drives sleep pressure; caffeine blocks its receptors | Adenosine accumulates during wakefulness and is a core part of the homeostatic sleep drive; caffeine is an adenosine-receptor antagonist (Porkka-Heiskanen et al., *Science* 1997; Huang et al., *Nat Neurosci* 2005) |
| Body clock lowest around 04:00; sleep urge strongest | Core body temperature reaches its daily minimum in the early morning (about 2 h before habitual waking, roughly 04:00–05:00), when circadian sleep drive peaks (Czeisler et al.; Dijk & Czeisler, *J Neurosci* 1995) |
| 24 h awake ≈ 0.10 % blood alcohol in performance | Performance after 24 h awake matched a BAC of about 0.10 % (Dawson & Reid, *Nature* 1997) |
| Over the legal driving limit | Most countries set 0.05 %; the US and UK (England/Wales) use 0.08 %. The on-screen scale shows the 0.05–0.08 % band |
| Amygdala ~60 % more reactive | After one night without sleep, amygdala response to negative images was about 60 % greater (Yoo et al., *Current Biology* 2007) |
| Hippocampus takes in ~40 % less new information | After a night without sleep, people formed about 40 % fewer new memories, with reduced hippocampal activity during learning (Yoo et al., *Nature Neuroscience* 2007) |
| Ghrelin up, leptin down | Short sleep lowers leptin and raises ghrelin, and increases appetite for carbohydrate-rich food (Spiegel et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine* 2004; Taheri et al., *PLoS Med* 2004) |
| Stress hormones and inflammation rise | Sleep loss raises evening cortisol and inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP (Leproult et al., *Sleep* 1997; Meier-Ewert et al., *JACC* 2004) |
| One sleepless night raised beta-amyloid ~5 % | PET imaging showed about a 5 % increase in beta-amyloid in the hippocampus and thalamus after one night of sleep deprivation (Shokri-Kojori et al., *PNAS* 2018). Sleep helps clear brain waste (Xie et al., *Science* 2013) |
| Microsleeps of a few seconds, eyes can stay open | Microsleeps last about 1–15 s, can happen with eyes open, and grow more frequent with sleep loss (Poudel et al., *NeuroImage* 2014) |
| A few seconds at highway speed ≈ a football field | 3 s at 110 km/h = 92 m; an American football field is 91.4 m (100 yd) |
| Hallucinations around 72 h | Perceptual distortions appear after 24–48 h; hallucinations and psychosis-like symptoms after about 72 h (Waters et al., *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 2018) |
| Randy Gardner, 1964, eleven days | 17-year-old high-school student in San Diego, 264 hours (about 11 days), as a science-fair project |
| Adults need 7–9 hours | Recommended for adults 18–64 (Hirshkowitz et al., National Sleep Foundation, *Sleep Health* 2015; AASM/SRS consensus 2015 says 7 or more) |
| No need to repay every lost hour; a night or two of deep sleep | Recovery sleep is deeper (more slow-wave sleep) rather than hour-for-hour; Gardner slept about 14 hours and recovered. Most effects of short-term loss reverse after one or two recovery nights |
