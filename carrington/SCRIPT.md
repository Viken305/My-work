# The Day the Sun Hit Us: script (draft for approval)

**Length:** about **2:20** (around 330 words at the Valdivia narration pace). Seven slides. Every figure below is
checked against the sources at the end. Items marked ⚠ are softened or worded carefully because the sources disagree.

| # | Slide | Narration | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook (0:00–0:18) | On the night of September 2nd, 1859, gold miners in the Rocky Mountains woke up and started making breakfast. It was one in the morning. The sky was so bright, they thought the sun was up. And in a way... it was. | Aurora over the miners' camp. Title card *THE DAY THE SUN Hit Us*. |
| 2 | The sketch (0:18–0:45) | The day before, in his observatory south of London, Richard Carrington was drawing sunspots. At 11:18 in the morning, two beads of blinding white light burst out of them. They lasted about five minutes. He had just seen the first solar flare ever recorded. | Telescope throws the Sun onto his card, beads A and B flare, pocket watch 11:18. |
| 3 | The crossing (0:45–1:05) | Its light reached us in eight minutes. But the Sun had also hurled a vast cloud of magnetised gas into space. Clouds like that usually take two to four days to reach Earth. This one took about seventeen and a half hours. And it was aimed at us. | Race card: light 8 min · normal cloud 2–4 days · this cloud 17.6 h. `17.6 HOURS` rides the cloud. |
| 4 | The shield (1:05–1:20) | It slammed into Earth's magnetic field and squeezed it. At Kew Observatory, the needles measuring the field were driven right off the scale. The biggest magnetic storm on record had begun. | Field lines crushed, `squeezed` on a field line, the Kew magnetogram running off the paper. |
| 5 | The aurora (1:20–1:45) | Then the sky caught fire. Auroras, usually seen near the poles, blazed over Cuba, Hawaii, even Panama. In the northeastern United States, people could read a newspaper by their light. | Globe with the aurora pushed to the tropics. Curtains over the Rockies. |
| 6 | The telegraph (1:45–2:05) | And the telegraph, the internet of 1859, went haywire. Wires sparked, some offices caught fire, and operators got electric shocks. Between Boston and Portland, two operators unplugged their batteries completely... and kept on sending messages, powered by the sky alone. | Poles crackling, sparks at the key, the real message on Morse tape: *WE ARE WORKING WITH THE AURORAL CURRENT*. The tape scorches. |
| 7 | The near miss (2:05–2:20) | Back then, it was a wonder. Today, a storm like that could knock out power grids and satellites. And in July 2012, one nearly did. A cloud just as strong left the Sun. Nine days earlier, it would have hit us. | The Sun's spin carries the active region away from Earth; the cloud hits STEREO-A instead. Today's lit night side below. |

## Fact-check notes
- **11:18 a.m., about five minutes, two beads, seen independently by Richard Hodgson.** Carrington (1859) and Hodgson
  (1859) in *MNRAS* 20. The first flare ever recorded.
- **17.6 hours.** The transit time from the flare to the storm's sudden commencement (Cliver & Svalgaard 2004). Normal
  CMEs take days; "two to four days" is the usual range. The speed was likely helped by an earlier CME clearing the
  path.
- **Light in eight minutes.** 8 min 20 s for 1 AU.
- **Biggest magnetic storm on record.** The Dst estimates range from about −850 to −1,750 nT (Tsurutani et al. 2003;
  later revisions are lower). We say "on record" and give no number. ⚠
- **Kew magnetograms off scale.** Stewart (1861), Phil. Trans. R. Soc.
- **Aurora in Cuba, Hawaii, Panama.** Hayakawa et al. (2016, 2018) compile records from Hawaii, Panama, Santiago and
  Cuba. A Colombian report exists but its interpretation is debated, so it is left out. ⚠
- **Read a newspaper.** Reported in the northeastern US (contemporary newspapers, collected by Loomis 1860; quoted by
  NASA).
- **Rocky Mountain miners making breakfast.** Contemporary report quoted by Wikipedia and NASA. Kept as "woke up and
  started making breakfast".
- **Telegraph sparks, fires, shocks.** Loomis (1861), *Am. J. Sci.*; Wikipedia summary ("in some cases giving telegraph
  operators electric shocks"). "Some offices caught fire" is worded softly. ⚠
- **Boston–Portland exchange.** Boston Traveler, 3 Sept 1859, quoted by Loomis. The words on the tape are the operator's
  own: "we are working with the auroral current". We don't state "two hours", which only weak sources give. ⚠
- **July 2012.** The 23 July 2012 CME was at least as strong as 1859 (Baker et al. 2013, *Space Weather*). It erupted on
  the Sun's far side and hit STEREO-A, about 121° ahead of Earth. The region had faced Earth about nine days earlier
  (Liu et al. 2014; UC Berkeley). NASA's summary says "one week earlier". We use nine days, which matches the
  geometry. ⚠
