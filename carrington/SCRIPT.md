# The Day the Sun Hit Us: final script

**Length:** 2:02 (seven slides). This is the narration as recorded (`script.py`). Every claim is checked against the
sources at the end. Items marked ⚠ were softened or reworded during the final fact check.

| # | Slide | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook + title | On the night of September 2nd, 1859, gold miners in the Rocky Mountains woke up, and started making breakfast. It was 1 a.m. The sky was so bright, they thought the sun was up. And in a way... it was. *(title: THE DAY THE SUN Hit Us)* |
| 2 | The sketch | The day before, in his observatory south of London, astronomer Richard Carrington was drawing sunspots. At 11:18 a.m., two beads of blinding white light burst out of them. In about 5 minutes, they were gone. He had just seen the first solar flare ever recorded. |
| 3 | The crossing | Its light reached Earth in about 8 minutes. But the sun had also hurled a vast cloud of magnetised gas into space. Clouds like that usually take a few days to reach us. This one took just 17.6 hours. And it was aimed right at Earth. |
| 4 | The shield | It slammed into Earth's magnetic field, and squeezed it. At Kew Observatory near London, the instruments recording the field were driven right off the scale. The biggest magnetic storm in recorded history had begun. |
| 5 | The aurora | Then the sky caught fire. Auroras, usually seen only near the poles, blazed over Cuba, Hawaii, and even Panama. In the northeastern United States, people could read a newspaper by their light. |
| 6 | The telegraph | And the telegraph, the internet of 1859, went haywire. Wires threw sparks, and some operators got electric shocks. Between Boston and Portland, two operators unplugged their batteries completely, and kept on sending messages, powered by the sky alone. |
| 7 | The near miss | Back then, it was a wonder. Today, a storm like that could knock out power grids and satellites. And in July 2012, one nearly did. A cloud at least as strong blasted off the sun. It missed us. But 9 days earlier, the spot it came from was facing Earth. |

## Changes made in the final fact check
- "Clouds like that usually take **2–4 days**" became "**a few days**": typical transit times range from about 1 to
  several days, so a fixed range overstated the precision.
- "about seventeen and a half hours" became "**17.6 hours**" to match Cliver & Svalgaard exactly.
- Kew "off the scale" is confirmed (the Kew and Greenwich magnetograms were both driven off scale; Cliver &
  Dietrich 2013, *J. Space Weather Space Clim.*).
- "Some offices caught fire" was dropped: the sparks and shocks are well reported, the fires less so.
- The tape shows the Boston operator's words "WE ARE WORKING WITH THE AURORAL CURRENT". The wording varies slightly
  between reprints, and this form appears in several (Loomis 1860 via later accounts).
- July 2012: the source region was about 141° west of the Earth-facing point, and STEREO-A (hit) was about 121° ahead
  of Earth. The region had faced Earth about nine days earlier (Wikipedia; Liu et al. 2014). The diagram shows the
  Sun's rotation, not Earth's orbit.
- The newspaper in slide 5 is a generic illustration ("EXTRA"), not a real 1859 paper.

## Fact-check notes
- **11:18 a.m., about five minutes, two beads, seen independently by Richard Hodgson.** Carrington (1859) and Hodgson
  (1859) in *MNRAS* 20. The first flare ever recorded.
- **17.6 hours.** The transit time from the flare to the storm's sudden commencement (Cliver & Svalgaard 2004). Typical
  CMEs take one to several days, so the narration says "a few days". The speed was likely helped by an earlier CME clearing the
  path.
- **Light in eight minutes.** 8 min 20 s for 1 AU.
- **Biggest magnetic storm on record.** The Dst estimates range from about −850 to −1,750 nT (Tsurutani et al. 2003;
  later revisions are lower). We say "on record" and give no number. ⚠
- **Kew magnetograms off scale.** Stewart (1861), Phil. Trans. R. Soc.; Cliver & Dietrich (2013): driven off scale at
  Kew and at Greenwich.
- **Aurora in Cuba, Hawaii, Panama.** Hayakawa et al. (2016, 2018) compile records from Hawaii, Panama, Santiago and
  Cuba. A Colombian report exists but its interpretation is debated, so it is left out. ⚠
- **Read a newspaper.** Reported in the northeastern US (contemporary newspapers, collected by Loomis 1860; quoted by
  NASA).
- **Rocky Mountain miners making breakfast.** Contemporary report quoted by Wikipedia and NASA. Kept as "woke up and
  started making breakfast".
- **Telegraph sparks and shocks.** Loomis (1861), *Am. J. Sci.*; Wikipedia summary ("in some cases giving telegraph
  operators electric shocks"). Fires are left out of the narration. ⚠
- **Boston–Portland exchange.** Boston Traveler, 3 Sept 1859, quoted by Loomis. The words on the tape are the operator's
  own: "we are working with the auroral current". We don't state "two hours", which only weak sources give. ⚠
- **July 2012.** The 23 July 2012 CME was at least as strong as 1859 (Baker et al. 2013, *Space Weather*). It erupted on
  the Sun's far side and hit STEREO-A, about 121° ahead of Earth. The region had faced Earth about nine days earlier
  (Wikipedia; Liu et al. 2014). NASA's summary says "one week earlier". We use nine days, which matches the
  geometry. ⚠
