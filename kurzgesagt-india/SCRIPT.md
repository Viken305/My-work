# India: From the First Cities to One in Six Humans (script, draft v1)

**Working title:** *India: How One in Six Humans Got Here*
**Alt titles:** *What Is India, Really?* · *India Explained: 4,500 Years in 6 Minutes*
**Length:** ~6:00 (about 900 words of narration in 7 parts)
**Tone:** curious, warm, balanced. It celebrates India's story without becoming promotional, and treats politics evenly.

> Every number below was checked against a source (list at the end). Figures marked † are current estimates that can change, so re-check them just before upload.

---

## PART 1: Hook (~0:35)

> One in every six people alive today lives in India.
>
> Almost one and a half billion people, speaking hundreds of languages, following almost every major religion on Earth, and voting in the largest elections in human history.
>
> It's a country that built some of the world's first cities, and recently landed a spacecraft near the south pole of the Moon.
>
> So how did India become India? And where is it going next?

**Visuals:** Earth turns to show the subcontinent. A crowd of tiny, diverse figures fills it: one in every six figures in a row lights up saffron. A ballot box. An ancient brick city morphs into a rocket on the Moon. The bird pops up with a "?".

---

## PART 2: Ancient India (~0:55)

> About four and a half thousand years ago, along the Indus River, people built cities with grid streets, brick houses and covered drains. The Indus Valley Civilisation was one of the earliest urban societies on the planet.
>
> Later, the Vedas were composed, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism. And new ideas kept being born here: Buddhism and Jainism too.
>
> Around two thousand three hundred years ago, the emperor Ashoka ruled most of the subcontinent. After a brutal war, he turned to Buddhism and carved messages of tolerance into stone pillars. The lions from one of those pillars are still India's national emblem today.
>
> And over the following centuries, Indian mathematicians developed the decimal number system, with zero as a number, that the whole world uses today.

**Visuals:** Mohenjo-daro-style city building itself brick by brick (grid streets, drains). A palm-leaf manuscript glowing. Three small symbols appear (Om, Dharma wheel, Jain hand). Ashoka's pillar rises; the lion capital zooms to the emblem. Digits 1–9 + 0 fly out and spread across a globe.

---

## PART 3: Empires, Colonial Rule and Freedom (~1:10)

> For centuries, India's spices, cotton and diamonds drew traders and conquerors. From the 1500s, the Mughal Empire ruled much of the north, leaving wonders like the Taj Mahal.
>
> Then came a company. In 1757, the British East India Company won the Battle of Plassey, and slowly took control of the subcontinent. After a huge uprising in 1857, the British Crown took over directly.
>
> British rule brought railways, but Indian thinkers argued it was draining the country's wealth, and it was scarred by devastating famines.
>
> Then a lawyer named Mohandas Gandhi led millions in non-violent resistance, alongside leaders like Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel and Subhas Chandra Bose. In 1930, Gandhi walked nearly four hundred kilometres to the sea, to make salt and defy a British tax.
>
> On the fifteenth of August, 1947, India became independent. But it was also divided. Pakistan was created, and in the chaos, more than ten million people were uprooted, and hundreds of thousands, perhaps up to two million, lost their lives.

**Visuals:** Spice ships on a sea map. Taj Mahal builds itself from white blocks. A company ledger book grows into a flag over the map. A train crosses; a scale tips with gold flowing out. Gandhi as a respectful silhouette walking, followed by a growing crowd, ending at the sea with salt crystals. Fireworks for 1947, then a line splits the map; tiny figures stream in two directions, with a quiet, sombre colour shift.

---

## PART 4: Building a Republic (~0:50)

> The new country had to be built from scratch. Its constitution was drafted by a committee led by B. R. Ambedkar, a scholar born into a caste once treated as "untouchable". It gave every adult the right to vote, and outlawed untouchability. It came into force on the twenty-sixth of January 1950, now Republic Day.
>
> Under India's first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru, the country built dams, steel plants and universities.
>
> The Green Revolution of the sixties and seventies helped a country that depended on food aid learn to feed itself.
>
> And in 1991, facing an economic crisis, India opened up its economy. Growth took off, and Indian software and services spread across the world.

**Visuals:** A huge book (the Constitution) opens; every adult figure gets a ballot. Dams, steel and a university rise. Wheat fields go from brown to golden. A locked gate swings open in 1991; growth line climbs; laptops and code fly outward over a globe.

---

## PART 5: Culture (~0:50)

> India's constitution lists twenty-two major languages, and hundreds more are spoken every day.
>
> Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism were all born here, and India is also home to one of the largest Muslim populations in the world.
>
> Yoga started here. So did the game that became chess. Indian film industries, from Bollywood to Tamil and Telugu cinema, make more films than any other country. And cricket is close to a religion of its own.
>
> Even the food tells a story. Chillies, now at the heart of so many Indian dishes, only arrived about five hundred years ago, from the Americas.

**Visuals:** A word "hello" morphs through scripts (Devanagari, Tamil, Bengali, Urdu, Gurmukhi…). Festival montage with lamps (Diwali), colours (Holi), crescent and lanterns (Eid), a star (Christmas). Chess piece from a chaturanga board. Film reel; cricket ball. A chilli sails across the ocean on a Portuguese ship and lands in a curry.

---

## PART 6: India Today, and Narendra Modi (~1:20)

> Today, India is the world's most populous country, and the fastest-growing major economy. It is already the fifth-largest economy on Earth, and on track to overtake Japan for fourth.
>
> In recent decades, hundreds of millions of people have escaped extreme poverty. Its digital payment system, UPI, handles more than twenty billion payments every month. And in 2023, India became the first country to land near the Moon's south pole.
>
> Since 2014, India has been led by Prime Minister Narendra Modi. Born in 1950 in a small town in Gujarat, he says he helped at his father's tea stall as a boy. He rose through the Hindu nationalist organisation, the RSS, and ran the state of Gujarat for more than twelve years.
>
> His government introduced a nationwide goods and services tax, built roads, railways and airports at record speed, opened bank accounts for hundreds of millions of people, and built more than a hundred million toilets. It also made bold and controversial moves, like suddenly cancelling most cash notes in 2016, and ending Jammu and Kashmir's special status in 2019.
>
> Supporters say he has made India more confident, modern and respected in the world. Critics worry about rising religious tensions, pressure on the press and opposition, and not enough jobs for young people.
>
> In 2024, he won a third term. But his party lost its outright majority, and now governs in a coalition.

**Visuals:** Growth chart with India's bar climbing past others (labelled "5th → 4th (projected)"). Phone tapping a QR code, with a counter ticking to "20+ BILLION / MONTH". Chandrayaan-3 lander touching down. Modi: a respectful, simple flat portrait card (no caricature), with a timeline beside it: Vadnagar → Gujarat CM 2001–14 → PM 2014. Policy icons pop in a grid (GST, highway, bank card, toilet), then two "controversial" icons (banknote with X, map pin). **Two equal columns:** "SUPPORTERS SAY" and "CRITICS SAY", same size, same colour weight, same screen time. 2024: a parliament hemicycle where his party's seats fall just short of the majority line, then coalition partners join.

---

## PART 7: Challenges, the Future, and Outro (~0:40)

> India's challenges are as big as its ambitions. Millions of young people need good jobs every year. Too few women are in paid work. Its cities have some of the worst air on Earth, and climate change brings heat waves and water shortages.
>
> But India is also young. Half of its people are under thirty.
>
> In a single lifetime, it went from a newly independent nation, poor and divided, to the largest democracy and one of the largest economies on the planet.
>
> What it becomes next will shape the lives of one in six humans, and very likely, the rest of us too.

**Visuals:** Four challenge icons (job briefcase, woman at work, smog over a skyline, a cracked dry field under a sun). A population pyramid with a wide young base glowing. Timeline 1947 → today. Zoom out from India to Earth, then to the bird watching a sunrise over the subcontinent. End card.

**Balance rules used for Part 6:**
- Achievements are stated as what the government *did* (verifiable facts).
- Opinions are attributed: "supporters say…", "critics worry…".
- Supporters and critics get the **same number of points, the same length and the same visual treatment**.
- No contested claims are stated as fact in the narrator's voice.
